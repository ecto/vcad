The CAM panel turns the part on screen into a CNC program and refuses to hand
it over until the program has been replayed against the part it is meant to
make. It is the same job API the desktop app drives through its C ABI and an
agent drives through the `cam` MCP tools — one implementation of "is this job
safe to run?", because a second one would be a second answer.

Open it from the workspace selector in the sidebar.

<Callout type="warning" title="A refused job has no G-code">
When verification fails, the answer carries `blocked: true` and **no G-code at
all** — not a disabled button, nothing to export. The panel shows why, in
machinist language, and which operation it landed in.
</Callout>

## Workflow

### 1 · Take the contour from the part

**Setup → Take contour from part** sections the part at the mid-height of its
own bounds and reads the boundary and openings off the section.

The contour comes out of the kernel, not out of the mesh on screen. The
evaluated scene's mesh is the *export* mesh, whose repair pass can move a
vertex by a few tenths where tangent faces meet; on the stator that was 0.4 mm,
a quarter of a slot mouth. The panel sections the B-rep's raw tessellation
instead and says which it used — "B-rep (exact)" is the one you want.

If the solid does not close at that Z, the panel says so and lists the gaps. It
does **not** heal the outline: a healed outline is a wall the part does not
have, and the fix belongs in the model.

### 2 · Stock, tool, material

Stock is a thickness, a margin, and optionally a sacrificial board beneath it.
Units are mm, Z is up, and the top of the stock is Z0.

**Recommend feeds** asks the kernel's material table for a feed, plunge, rpm,
stepdown and stepover for this cutter in this material on this class of machine.
On a trim router the `S` word does nothing, so the recommendation also carries
the **dial position to set by hand** — the number in the program is not the
speed the spindle will run at.

Hazards and cautions attached to the material (FR4 dust, copper needing
lubricant, depth limits under Ø1 mm) are shown with the recommendation.

### 3 · Operations

The operations are derived from the outline rather than typed in, by three
rules:

| The hole | Becomes | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Ø ≤ cutter Ø | **Refused, by name** | No toolpath fits. The fix is a smaller cutter, so the panel says which holes and how big |
| cutter Ø < Ø < 2 × cutter Ø | **Helical bore** | Too narrow to spiral an offset pass inside |
| Ø ≥ 2 × cutter Ø | **Cut out** (inside contour) | The cutter can work within the opening |

Everything else inside the part is an opening, cut out so the waste drops free.
Select an opening to keep the slug instead, as a **pocket**. Holes of one
diameter share a single row and are bored in one operation each.

The outside profile runs last and carries **tabs** — three, 4 mm wide, 1 mm
tall by default (never more than half the stock) — so the part is still held
when the cut that frees it finishes.

### 4 · Build and verify

**Build and verify job** posts the whole job, posts the G-code, and replays
*that G-code* against the part. Eight checks come back, each with a number:

| Check | Measures |
|---|---|
| Cuts into the part | How far any cutting move entered the part |
| Wall left standing | Unswept area in the wall bands, and how proud |
| Rapids below the stock top | Rapids travelling in XY through material |
| Depth against the stock | Deepest Z, and metal left under the part |
| Holding tabs | Tabs cut, and passes that step over them |
| Travel and sweep | Swept extents, against machine travel |
| Pieces that come free | Stock the job frees completely |
| Plunges into uncut metal | Vertical entries with nowhere for chips to go |

Whether a failed check blocks or warns is the oracle's decision, reported in
`blocked_by` and `warnings`. Blockers are shown as sentences — "The cut goes
3.000 mm past the stock underside at X 12.9, Y 12.0" rather than `depth`.

### 5 · Export

**Export G-code** turns on when all three hold:

1. the job was built and the oracle did not refuse it;
2. there is a program (a refused job has none);
3. every warning has been acknowledged, one checkbox at a time.

Editing anything — a feed, the tool, the stock — drops the acknowledgements,
because they were read about a different job.

## Preview

The Ops and Verify tabs draw the moves the post emitted, looking down at the
stock: **cut** moves solid, **rapid** moves dashed, the part outline dotted.
Selecting an operation row draws that operation alone and greys the rest.

The preview is the program's own move list, not a second computation of where
the cutter goes.

## Cycle time

The estimate is acceleration-aware — it accounts for the machine's acceleration
and junction deviation rather than dividing distance by feed. It is still a
prediction. Cycle time, feeds and cutter fit stay predictions until a part comes
off the machine and is measured.

## What the panel does not do yet

The desktop app is ahead of the browser on the machine-side half of the
pipeline. The web panel does not (yet) drive a machine, probe, trace bounds,
place clamps as keep-outs, drag tabs, import a DXF to machine against, or run
multi-tool jobs — it builds one job for one cutter and exports it. The
verification and the refusal are the same in both.

## Feeds and speeds

Recommendations come from the kernel's material table: a chipload for the
material at a reference diameter, scaled to the cutter and derated for the
machine's rigidity class, with the surface speed that implies.

<Callout type="warning" title="Verify on your machine">
Feed and speed recommendations are starting points. Verify against your
machine's rigidity and workholding before running any program. Start
conservative.
</Callout>
